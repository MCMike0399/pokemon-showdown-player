\section{Fourteen campaigns: from prediction to playing strength}
\label{sec:campaigns}

The campaign ledger extends the early evidence through 31 October. A campaign
is a fixed research question and protocol, not necessarily a model training
run. Some fit networks, some change simulator search, and some only compare
fixed alternatives on the ladder. Table~\ref{tab:campaign-ledger} records the
outcomes; the following subsections explain the mechanisms and the lessons.
The notation $g/\ell$ denotes discordant gains and losses, never the candidate's
ordinary win--loss record.

\subsection{C1--C2: outcome prediction is not counterfactual ranking}
The first search-learning loop finally trained a component used by turn
search rather than a sampled actor used only in a separate self-play system.
Completed live ledgers were reconstructed into positions and labelled with
verified results; CPU self-play added omniscient examples. The MPS trainer
split by game, calibrated on older live games and reserved the newest 40\%
as a test. Five-seed ensembles reduced fitting variance and were distilled
into an engine-readable network.

The strongest reported C1 candidate improved live-test log loss from .538
to .497 and AUC from .793 to .831. Yet its development $12/11$ and
confirmation $9/5$ ledgers were stopped without a pass. Earlier gates
aggregated $58/71$. The live policy therefore stayed on the hand evaluation
through the 1,018-game review. Fitting who eventually won does not identify
which alternative action would have caused a better outcome.

Calibration put learned values on the hand evaluation's scale. If
$\Prob(\mathrm{win}\mid h)=\sigma(ah+b)$ and the network emits logit $z$
with live temperature $T$, the converted score was
$u=(z/T-b)/a$, clipped to $[-20,20]$, and the blend was
$(1-\beta)h+\beta u$. This avoids silently changing the units of opponent
softmax temperatures or missing-world penalties. It does not remove the
observational nature of the labels.

C2 addressed ranking directly. Sibling groups held the reply and chance
stream fixed while changing one side's action and playing out the remainder.
Of 6,400 groups, 2,629 had unequal outcomes and informed the metric. Hand
pair accuracy was .6498; the best of 61 nets reached .6553, difference .0055
with a group-bootstrap interval $[-.0029,.0134]$, without adjustment for
selecting that net. The metric agreed with only 8 of 15 historical gate
orderings (Spearman $-.029$). It was not validated as a promotion shortcut.
Selective second-turn search separately failed at $53/43$, despite 47.48\%
more simulations per decision. Pseudo-move errors voided six pairs; even
counting all six as gains would not have passed the fixed test.

\subsection{C3--C4: learn what ladder opponents actually do}
The original gate opponent was search piloting real teams. Real team sets
did not make its decisions human. Public replays supported a separate
imitation opponent and new preview models. About 150,000 replay games were
collected; the first move and preview fits used about 10,600 and 12,600
respectively. Later analyses used filtered, rated subsets rather than a
single universal corpus count.

For a legal joint-action set $\A$, the move model assigns logits
$f_\psi(h,a)$. An executed public log can leave one slot's intended move
unknown after a flinch or knockout. With consistent label set
$C\subseteq\A$, the loss is
\begin{equation}
  -\log\sum_{a\in C}\frac{e^{f_\psi(h,a)}}{\sum_{u\in\A}e^{f_\psi(h,u)}}.
  \label{eq:human-marginal}
\end{equation}
About 19\% of labels needed this marginalization. Inputs combine 146
position features, 70 joint-action features and move/species embeddings.
Whole games stay within one split. Reveals later in the replay can recover
facts about the acting player's own sets for retrospective training; they
are not facts available to our live opponent model at that earlier turn.

The preview imitation is listwise over 90 plans:
$\binom62\binom42=90$, treating both leads and bench as unordered pairs.
The transport maps the selected plan back into a legal ordered preview.
This differs from the actor's 180 ordered-lead classes in
Proposition~\ref{prop:preview}. A separate matchup-value classifier learns
$P(\mathrm{win}\mid\text{own six},\text{opposing six},\text{plan})$ from
human outcomes; it is an observational predictor, not an identified causal
value for all unplayed plans.

On 957 identifiable live opponent actions, the deployed equilibrium/greedy
model achieved top-1 .176 and NLL 3.70; the .8-human/.2-deployed mixture
achieved .264 and 2.82. C3 nevertheless failed its gate. C4's imitation
preview passed development and failed fresh confirmation. The value preview
passed both ($81/52$, then $87/62$) and was deployed on 14 October. Its early
live segment was $1236\pm28$ over 635 games versus the preceding
$1206\pm19$ over 1,325. That chronological comparison suggests a modest
improvement but is not randomized evidence for the preview alone.

\subsection{C5--C8: risk attitude, horizon and an imperfect gate}
C5 diagnosed a horizon effect: Protect preserves material for a leaf even
when the next turn remains lost. A live logistic calibration over 12,896
positions gave $P(\mathrm{win})=\sigma(.801h-.430)$. The deployed leaf became
\begin{equation}
  L(h)=\frac4{.801}\,\sigma(.801h-.430).
  \label{eq:sigmoid-leaf}
\end{equation}
Although monotone in $h$, the transformation changes decisions when values
are averaged over stochastic outcomes. Its convex region below the sigmoid
midpoint can favour an uncertain escape over certain slow defeat; its concave
region above it can favour preserving a win. The empirical calibration is
not a guarantee that every counterfactual leaf is a true win probability.
A second turn re-evaluates the best Protect and non-Protect lines when the
world's material value is below $-1$. Speed-order observations constrain
usage draws, with exclusions for priority ambiguity and mid-turn Speed changes.
The equilibrium weight was changed from .5 to .25 as part of the bundle.

\paragraph{The amendment must remain visible.}
Leaf-only failed ($36/32$). The full bundle passed development
($44/23$, $p\approx.007$), missed confirmation ($52/38$, $p\approx.085$),
and showed no significant harm against standard search ($29/17$).
The original automatic promoter declined. After the stages finished,
amendment~1 pooled confirmation with the different standard-opponent stage
($81/55$, nominal $p\approx.016$) and promoted anyway. This was a deliberate
post-results exception, not a pass of the original preregistered rule.
A prospective live rule required at least 300 games and rollback below the
C4 performance segment. C5 cleared it; the eventual segment was
$1287\pm21$ over 1,169 games. A room-scoping browser repair shipped in the
same deployment, so the segment cannot isolate the search bundle's effect.

C6 enlarged worlds and screening separately; neither passed. This rejects
those fixed candidates under their gate, not the general importance of
sampling noise. C7 combined boost/Speed/evasion/weather valuation, broader
Protect continuation, endgame depth, Mega avoidance and a corpus back-pair
prior. PLAY failed $45/52$; the larger preview ensemble failed $61/88$.
C8's public-history scouting achieved high item precision in diagnostics but
only $17/14$ in its gate. None of those options became the default.

The gate itself was then audited. Its argmax imitation Protected on 29\% of
actions versus about 12\% for ladder humans, switched about 2.8\% versus
8.4\%, and lost to our search roughly three quarters of the time. Requiring
two significant stages with repeated early futility made modest gains hard
to detect. Better predictions and changed move frequencies were useful
candidate-development evidence, but inadequate substitutes for target-domain
wins. C9 moved the experiment onto the ladder.

\subsection{C9--C11: direct experimentation and focused candidates}
C9 randomized four binary factors in balanced 16-game blocks. Wider Protect
continuation, boost/Speed/evasion terms, a back-rate bench prior and online
scouting each had a 0-versus-+40-Elo sequential comparison. Every factor
stopped at H0 (Table~\ref{tab:c9-final}). The early boost estimate of +19 Elo
at 1,223 games shrank to +1.3 at its 1,581-game stopping decision. H0 here
chooses zero over the specified +40 alternative; it does not establish
exactly zero effect, harmfulness or equivalence. The completed campaign
ledger contains 1,582 games because a result arrived after the stopping
snapshot. Decisions must use the saved first decision, not a later summary.

C10 froze two narrower candidates and used a pooled 800-pair offline rule,
with only rejection looks at 400 and 600 and one final significance test.
The preview guard penalized Intimidate leads in selected opposing matchups
only among plans within .025 of the incumbent's top probability. It stopped
at $8/10$ over 400 pairs. The combined screening/endgame candidate trained
on 1,500 baseline games (1,186,801 action rows); MPS fitting took 8.1 seconds.
Its held-out final-choice retention was 99.11\% and estimated simulation
saving 15.12\%. Those engineering metrics qualified the fixed bundle for a
strength test, which ended $108/106$, $p=.47276$, over 785 completed and
15 void pairs. Neither candidate reached the required fresh live test.

C11 repaired Illusion and Recharge reconstruction and explored a bounded
preview-logit residual. Its 8,868 examples were split chronologically
70/15/15 by whole game. A 32-unit residual, capped at $\pm.35$ logits,
trained in 1.53 seconds on MPS. Validation improvement .001055 missed the
required .002, and test log loss worsened from .647704 to .648898. The
candidate remained disabled rather than tuning against the failed test set.
Repairs and scheduling improvements are discussed in
Section~\ref{sec:engineering}; they are not claimed as measured Elo gains.

\subsection{C12: a shared Bayesian root strategy}
\label{sec:belief-root}
The baseline commits to one root action, but it solves hidden worlds
separately and averages an action only across screens that retained it.
This is selection-dependent coverage: an action worth 1 in its only retained
world and 0 in five omitted worlds receives $1-.5=.5$, beating an action
worth .4 everywhere, although its full-world mean is only $1/6$.
Replacing this rule requires completing comparable rows, not filling missing
payoffs with guessed constants.

C12 nominated up to 24 actions, completed each against every retained reply
in every successful world, and reused the original serialized states and
chance streams. For posterior weights $p_w$ and complete matrices $M_w$,
it solved the one-sided private-type game
\begin{equation}
  V^*=\max_{x\in\Delta(\A)}V(x),\qquad
  V(x)=\sum_w p_w\min_{b\in\B_w}\sum_a x_a M_w[a,b].
  \label{eq:belief-root}
\end{equation}
One $x$ commits our joint action across worlds; the opponent may respond to
its own private type. Averaging matrices before taking the minimum would
erase that information advantage. The implementation uses equal weights,
nonnegative cumulative regrets, linearly weighted averages, and a recorded
primal--dual gap
\begin{equation}
  G=\max_a\sum_w p_w(M_w\bar y_w)_a
       -\sum_w p_w\min_b(\bar x^\top M_w)_b.
\end{equation}

\begin{proposition}[Scope of a shared-root certificate]
For any complete finite matrices and probability vectors $\bar x,\bar y_w$,
$V(\bar x)\le V^*\le U$, where
$U=\max_a\sum_wp_w(M_w\bar y_w)_a$. Thus $G=U-V(\bar x)$ bounds the
suboptimality of $\bar x$ in this sampled game.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The first inequality follows by maximization over $x$. For any $x$, each
minimum over replies is at most the expectation under $\bar y_w$, so
$V(x)\le\sum_wp_wx^\top M_w\bar y_w\le U$. Maximize over $x$.
\end{proof}

Bounded exploitation moved along the line from $\bar x$ toward a pure best
response to the existing opponent prior, choosing the largest movement with
$V(x)\ge V(\bar x)-.1$. Concavity of $V$ makes the feasible portion of this
line an interval, permitting bisection. This is a restricted line search,
not the full constrained optimal-exploitation solution. The principle of
trading model exploitation against worst-case robustness has antecedents in
restricted Nash responses \citep{rnash2007}. A separate RNG sampled the
entire joint action, retained across submission retries. Existing selective
deeper estimates replaced their actual matrix cells before the shared solve;
other cells remained one-turn leaves. This fixed bundle changed both coverage
and the old scalar correction, so it was not an isolated test of randomization.

On 48 frozen positions the diagnostic gaps were all below .01 (median
.00798), and the extra simulations were 13.4\%. Twelve fresh adaptive
contexts had legal choices and no root fallback; median latency rose from
5.18 to 6.26 seconds. These were engineering checks. The frozen strength
panel rotated incumbent search, argmax imitation and sampled imitation,
using both sides. It stopped at 400 pairs with $46/59$, $p=.91422$,
14.29\% more simulations per decision, and no live confirmation.

The negative result does not contradict the certificate. The guarantee
concerns sampled types, retained replies and approximate leaf payoffs.
Opponent sets are imperfect, the opponent is effectively informed about
our set, and deeper continuations can still depend on determinized hidden
information. Depth-limited imperfect-information solving generally needs
richer continuation constraints than one scalar full-state value
\citep{brown2018depth}. C12 improved the definition and solution of the
surrogate root game without establishing stronger play in the target game.

\subsection{C13--C14: change the team, then test its preview}
C13 reconsidered the fixed team after search campaigns had not broken the
plateau. Human corpus estimates were weighted equally by player to avoid
letting prolific strong accounts dominate team rankings. The 111,564 rated
games / 197,708 eligible sides showed wide team-effect intervals, not a
proven universally superior six. Live matchup audits suggested weaknesses
against Fairy attackers and Competitive/Defiant teams. Those exploratory
patterns motivated candidates; their estimated deficits were not added up
as a forecast of a team's improvement.

The tournament-derived Helm rain candidate retained much of the Recife core,
adding Gengar and Rillaboom; Mev combined Pelipper rain and Charizard-Y sun
with Archaludon, Grimmsnarl, Venusaur and Garchomp. Imported moves, items,
abilities and natures followed the attributed sheets; hidden stat points
used modal usage spreads. This distinction matters: an attributed team
archetype is not a claim to know the tournament player's exact hidden stats.
Both candidates passed simulator validation. The randomized king-of-the-hill
comparison is reported in Section~\ref{sec:live-method}.

After Mev won, its preview still used Archaludon and Grimmsnarl in every
recorded plan, Charizard in 95\%, Pelipper in 87\%, Garchomp in 16\% and
Venusaur in 3\% of the 885-game diagnostic. The same four occurred in 83\%.
In 1,046 public games from 298 players with the exact six, Venusaur was seen
in 42\% and Garchomp in 53\%. Public appearance is a lower bound on being
brought; it is not an exact counterpart of the bot's private preview record.
Nevertheless, Venusaur's absence identifies a plausible unused sun mode.

C14 fixes Mev and the turn search, comparing pvalue with
\code{preview-1455f4a55d18112a:argmax}. On the same 885 opposing previews,
that imitation would bring Venusaur in 63\% and lead Charizard--Venusaur in
558 games. This is a counterfactual plan-frequency diagnostic, not a
counterfactual win estimate. The experiment began on 31 October with the
same 0/+40-Elo, 300--3,000-game rule. At the paper cutoff only nine games
had finished: no decision is permitted, and no improvement is claimed.
